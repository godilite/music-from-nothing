Music From Nothing

How to write a 182-second cold, epic film score with no instruments, no samples and no music software, by understanding what sound is and telling Claude Code exactly what to compute.

A tutorial. Every example plays in your browser, computed live from the formulas on this page. The images were generated by code too.

I majored in physics in science education at the University of Calabar. One of the things I learned was harmonics, along with essentially all of the other BSc material on waves. Now I'm curious about how machines use them.

So I set myself a project. I wanted a theme like the one from Game of Thrones, except mine: low strings, war drums, cold air. A stock track had already got one of my videos taken down, so downloading something else felt like the wrong answer. The result is one JavaScript file with no dependencies. It computes about 17.5 million numbers, one pair for each 48,000th of a second, and writes them to disk. Those numbers are the music.

This article explains why that works, from air pressure up to orchestration. It assumes you can read code, and it does not assume you can read music. It goes deep, because the point is to understand the machine well enough to direct it. Use the labs as you go. A formula you have heard is much harder to forget.

The surprise for anyone who studied physics is how directly it carries over. A computer has no concept of a violin or a drum. It only has the mathematics of waves, written out as loops. Here is how familiar physics shows up in this project's code:

What a physics course usually leaves out is the last step. Those equations describe sound continuously, and a machine has to turn them into a finite list of numbers without breaking them. Part 3 covers that step, and it introduces its own physics-like trap, aliasing. The rest of the article builds from those foundations up to a finished score.

First, the obvious questionWhy not just use Suno?

Suno and tools like it can give you a finished track from one sentence in under a minute, with convincing strings and even vocals. If all you need is a good track today, use one. This method sounds more synthetic and takes far longer.

I went the long way because I wanted to learn what happens behind the scenes. What is music, physically? How does a computer make sound with no instrument? A prompt box would have handed me a song and left those questions exactly where they were. It wouldn't show me why a detuned sawtooth sounds like a section of strings, or what makes a room sound like a room. Getting a track was never the whole goal. Understanding how it gets made was.

Working this way also has some practical side effects, which I only came to appreciate afterwards:

Control over every note. In code, "put a drum hit at 38.037 seconds, on the word struck" is a single line, and "move only this note" means changing a single number. When the edit changes, the music recomposes itself to fit. A generated track arrives as a finished recording, and the usual way to change it is to generate again.

Everything in it is visible. The track is one readable file with no samples and no trained model behind it. Generators learn from large amounts of existing music, and how that training is licensed has been contested in court. Warner settled with Suno in November 2025 and moved to a licensing deal, while other disputes have continued.

No plan or service to depend on. On Suno's pricing page, the free plan has no commercial rights and the paid plans, from $8 a month, include commercial use, subject to its terms. Those terms have been changing as the licensing deals arrive, so check them yourself if you go that way. This file just runs on my laptop.

This doesn't make my track legally bulletproof. Claude Code wrote much of this code and drafted the melody with me, and copyright law in the US and elsewhere generally looks for human authorship, so how much protection AI-assisted work gets is unsettled. The more of the melody and decisions you make yourself, the better your position should be. Ask a lawyer before you build a business on it. Part 8 comes back to this.

Part 1What sound physically is

Sound is a travelling disturbance in air pressure. Something pushes air, the air molecules collide with their neighbours, and a region of slightly higher pressure moves outward, followed by a region of slightly lower pressure. The molecules themselves barely go anywhere. Only the pattern travels, at about 343 m/s in air at 20 °C. In one dimension, small pressure changes p obey the wave equation:

∂²p/∂t² = c² ∂²p/∂x²

Two facts from this equation carry the rest of the article. It is linear, so two sounds in the same air simply add. And at any single point, such as your eardrum, the entire sound reduces to one function of time, p(t): a single number that changes from moment to moment. A symphony reaching one ear is still one wiggling line. If you can compute that line and make a speaker cone follow it, you have made the sound. No instrument is needed in that chain.

The range is huge. The quietest sound a young ear detects near 1 kHz is about 20 micropascals, and a pressure a million times larger is loud enough to hurt. That is why we use a logarithmic scale, decibels, where 20 dB means ten times the pressure. Your ear responds to frequencies from about 20 Hz to 20 kHz, with the top end falling as you age.

What the ear does with it

The eardrum and middle ear pass the vibration into the cochlea, a coiled fluid-filled tube containing the basilar membrane. That membrane is stiff at one end and floppy at the other, so each frequency makes a different place along it vibrate most. About 3,500 inner hair cells along its length turn that motion into nerve signals. In effect, your ear runs a physical frequency analysis before your brain receives anything. This is why the next idea, breaking a sound into frequencies, describes hearing as well as mathematics.

The simplest possible p(t) is a sine wave. Its only properties are amplitude A, frequency f and starting phase φ:

p(t) = A · sin(2π f t + φ)

The left panel shows pressure against time. The right panel shows how much energy sits at each frequency, on a logarithmic axis. A pure sine is a single spike. It is also dull, and nothing in nature sounds quite like it. Keep both panels in view as we add things.

Part 2Pitch, timbre and Fourier's idea

Play the same note on a cello and a trumpet and you can tell them apart instantly. They share a pitch, so what differs? Joseph Fourier showed that any periodic function can be written as a sum of sines at whole-number multiples of a base frequency:

p(t) = Σn=1…∞ an sin(2π n f t + φn)

The base frequency f is the fundamental. The multiples 2f, 3f, 4f are the harmonics. Hermann von Helmholtz argued in the 1860s that the balance of those amplitudes is the main source of what we call tone colour, or timbre. Pitch is largely set by the repetition rate, and timbre by the recipe. Vibrating strings and air columns naturally produce near-harmonic series, because they can only hold whole numbers of half-waves. For a string of length L, tension T and mass per unit length μ, the standing waves are:

fn = (n / 2L) √(T / μ)

That classroom result is the reason a whole-number series is the right recipe for a computed string. The code never models the length, tension or mass. It jumps straight to the frequencies they would produce.

A bowed string behaves roughly like a sawtooth wave: the bow drags the string, then it slips back quickly. A sawtooth has every harmonic, with amplitude falling as 1/n:

saw(t) ∝ Σn (1/n) sin(2π n f t)

That sum is the strings in this score. Here is the inner loop from score.mjs, and it is exactly that formula:

for (let h = 1; h < bright && f * h < 9000; h++) s += Math.sin(phase[k] * h) / h

Move the slider from 1 to 40. You are building an instrument from pure sines, and on the right you can see each harmonic arrive. Pitch stays put while the colour changes from flute-like to buzzy. With only odd harmonics the sound turns hollow, closer to a clarinet, for the same physical reason that a tube closed at one end favours odd modes.

Your brain is doing real work here as well. If you remove the fundamental and keep the harmonics, you still hear the same pitch. This is the missing fundamental, and it is why a small speaker that cannot produce 55 Hz can still suggest a bass note. Pitch is something your auditory system infers from the pattern. It is not a simple read-out of the lowest frequency present.

Part 3Turning air into numbers

A computer cannot store a continuous function, so it stores values of p(t) at regular intervals. This is sampling. The sampling theorem, associated with Nyquist and Shannon, says a signal containing only frequencies below fs/2 can be perfectly reconstructed from samples taken at rate fs. Those stair-steps you may have seen in diagrams are misleading, because after reconstruction the output is smooth. This score uses 48,000 samples per second, so the limit, called the Nyquist frequency, is 24 kHz, above human hearing.

Break the rule and frequencies do not vanish. They fold back as false tones, which is called aliasing. A component at frequency f above the limit appears at:

falias = | f − fs · round(f / fs) |

So a 30 kHz harmonic at a 48 kHz sampling rate is heard at 18 kHz. The obvious way to code a sawtooth, a ramp that resets, has harmonics that never stop, so it always aliases. That is why the score adds up its harmonics by hand and stops at 9 kHz. It costs far more computing, and it stays clean.

Listen to the naive version as the pitch climbs. You will hear strange tones sliding the wrong way, and those are harmonics folding back from above the limit. The band-limited version only rises. Real synthesisers use cheaper methods such as wavetables and BLIT or BLEP oscillators. Summing sines directly is simple and correct, and it is fine when you render offline with no deadline.

How finely to store each number

Each sample is rounded to a limited precision, which adds a small error called quantisation noise. For an N-bit signal at full scale, the signal-to-noise ratio is about:

SNR ≈ 6.02 N + 1.76 dB

At 16 bits that is about 98 dB. The score does all of its calculations in 32-bit floats, then converts to 16-bit integers at the very end. Floats let the mix go far over the final limit in the middle of the work without being damaged.

A WAV file is barely a format at all: a 44-byte header followed by raw numbers. You can check the arithmetic on the real file. 182 seconds × 48,000 samples × 2 channels × 2 bytes = 34,944,000 bytes. Add the 44-byte header and you get 34,944,044, which is the exact size of score.wav on my disk. The script writes that header itself, by hand, with no audio library.

Part 4What music is

There is no agreed definition of music. The composer Edgard Varèse's phrase "organised sound" is a good working one, and it tells us what to study: how sounds are organised in pitch and in time, and why some of that organisation moves people.

Pitch: why these notes

Pitch perception is roughly logarithmic: going up an octave doubles the frequency, and it feels like the same step wherever you start. Western music divides the octave into 12 equal ratios. Using MIDI note numbers, where 69 is the A at 440 Hz, this gives the single most useful formula in this article. It is also a line of the script:

f(m) = 440 · 2(m − 69) / 12

Why 12? Because 27/12 ≈ 1.4983 lands within about 2 cents (2% of a semitone) of the simple ratio 3:2, called a perfect fifth, and several other simple ratios also come close. It is a compromise that works in every key, and most intervals are slightly out of tune with their pure versions as a result.

Consonance: why some notes feel right together

When two sine tones sit close in frequency, they interfere and the ear hears them as rough. Plomp and Levelt measured this in 1965 and found that roughness peaks when the tones are about a quarter of a critical bandwidth apart. A critical band is the frequency range the ear partly treats as one channel, about 100 Hz wide at low frequencies and about 20% of the frequency higher up. Real notes have many harmonics, so two notes clash wherever their harmonics nearly collide. In a 3:2 fifth, many harmonics land exactly on top of each other, so there is little roughness. In a semitone, they collide all the way up.

Culture matters too, so this is not the whole story. A 2016 study in Nature (McDermott and colleagues) found that Tsimane' people in Bolivia, with little exposure to Western music, could hear the difference between consonant and dissonant chords and did not prefer the consonant ones. So the physics of roughness is universal, while what feels pleasant is partly learned.

Watch the left panel while you switch between intervals. A pure fifth makes a stable repeating shape. A semitone produces slow pulsing, called beats. Then try the same intervals an octave lower using the register control, where the ear's bands are relatively wider and the clash gets muddier. This is why bass parts stay on wide intervals, and it is why this score's lowest voices play only roots and fifths.

Time: metre and expectation

Rhythm gives the brain something to predict. Leonard Meyer (1956) and David Huron (2006) developed the idea that much of music's emotional force comes from expectation being built, delayed, fulfilled or broken. A steady pulse creates a very strong prediction. When a loud drum lands precisely where you expected it, there is a physical sense of arrival.

This score is in 3/4, three beats to a bar, at about 96 beats per minute. A bar lasts 1.877 seconds. Three-beat metre has a lilt, a circular feel, where four-beat metre marches. Pairing a waltz-like metre with a low, heavy sound gives it a stately, circling, slightly fatalistic quality. That is a large part of the mood I was asking for.

Part 5Building a string section from a formula

A sawtooth is still a machine sound. Everything that makes it feel like a bowed string happens over time. These are the four ideas inside the score's bowed() function.

Phase accumulation

The naive oscillator sin(2π f t) breaks the moment the frequency changes, because the phase jumps. What matters is that phase is the running integral of frequency:

φ(t) = ∫0t 2π f(τ) dτ

Discretely, you keep a running total and add a little to it each sample. Vibrato and pitch slides then just work, with no clicks:

phase[k] += (2 * Math.PI * f * (1 + d) * v) / SR

Ensemble: many players, never quite together

One saw sounds like one synth. A section sounds big because its players are slightly out of tune with each other. The score uses three copies, detuned by −0.4%, 0% and +0.5%, which is about −7 and +9 cents. Two tones separated by Δf beat at Δf Hz, so each harmonic pulses at its own rate, and higher harmonics pulse faster. That constantly moving spectrum is what your ear reads as a group of players. Each voice also starts at a random phase, so no two notes begin identically.

Envelope: how the note starts and ends

Your ear identifies instruments heavily from their onset. If you cut the attack off a recorded instrument, it becomes surprisingly hard to name. The score multiplies each note by an envelope: a linear rise, a slow swell standing in for bow pressure, and an exponential tail after release:

const env = (t < attack ? t / attack : 1)
          * (t > dur ? Math.exp(-(t - dur) * 9) : 1)
          * (.85 + .15 * Math.sin(t * 2.1))

The pulse uses a 25 ms attack, so it bites. The melody uses 300 ms, so it swells. The drones use 2.4 seconds, so you notice them only after they have arrived.

Filter: the body of the instrument

Wood and air absorb high frequencies, and a raw saw has far too many of them. A low-pass filter removes them. The simplest digital version is one line, a moving average that remembers its last output:

y[n] = y[n−1] + a · (x[n] − y[n−1]),   a ≈ 2π fc / fs

It rolls off at 6 dB per octave. The score runs two in series for 12 dB per octave and sets the cutoff fc to 7 times the note's frequency. Tying it to the note keeps the same tone colour up and down the range, which is roughly how a real instrument body behaves. A fixed cutoff would make low notes bright and high notes dull.

Vibrato: the sign of a human

Vibrato is a small, slow change in pitch, here at 5.2 Hz. A real player does not vibrate instantly, so the score fades it in over the first 0.4 seconds:

const v = 1 + vib * Math.min(1, t / .4) * Math.sin(2 * Math.PI * 5.2 * t)

Set attack to its shortest, cutoff low and vibrato to zero, and you have a synth. Raise each one in turn and listen for where it starts sounding played. It will not fool a string player. Synthesising a truly realistic violin needs physical modelling, such as digital waveguides, or real samples. For a film-like bed under narration, this is enough.

Part 6Drums and wind

So far everything has been harmonic. Drums and wind are not, and they need different maths.

A drum is a falling sine and a burst of noise

Hitting a drum skin stretches it and raises its pitch for an instant. The pitch drops as the skin settles. Modes of a circular membrane are also inharmonic (they follow zeros of Bessel functions), which is why a drum has no clear note. The score ignores that full complexity and captures the two things your ear notices most: a sine that falls quickly from about 116 Hz to 46 Hz, plus a very short burst of filtered noise for the impact:

f(t) = 46 + 70 e−28 t   (Hz)
const s = (Math.sin(ph) * Math.exp(-t / decay) + nz * 1.6 * Math.exp(-t * 40)) * gain

Notice that the phase is accumulated again, because the frequency is changing.

Noise, and where randomness comes from

Wind is noise, random numbers with energy at every frequency. Code cannot be truly random without outside help, so the score uses a Lehmer generator, one of the oldest and simplest pseudo-random methods, with the constants Park and Miller recommended:

let seed = 7
const rand = () => { seed = (seed * 16807) % 2147483647; return seed / 2147483647 * 2 - 1 }

Raw noise sounds like static. Wind sounds like wind because the air resonates at frequencies that drift. So the noise passes through a state-variable filter whose centre frequency wanders on slow sine waves between roughly 0 and 780 Hz, and the output is panned gently from side to side. Only two state variables are involved, b1 and b2, and the sound comes almost entirely from that moving resonance.

Part 7Building a room

Sound in the real world arrives once directly and then thousands of times off walls. Without that, synthesised audio sounds like it is inside your skull. A computer can build a room from delays. Manfred Schroeder described the classic method in 1962, and this score uses a small version of it.

A comb filter feeds a delayed copy of the signal back into itself with a gain g below 1, producing a train of fading echoes:

H(z) = z−M / (1 − g z−M)

The time for its echoes to fall by 60 dB, called T60, follows from the delay d and gain:

T60 = −3 d / log10(g)

With g = 0.8 and delays of 29.7 to 45.1 ms, that gives about 0.9 to 1.4 seconds, a medium-sized room. Four combs run in parallel, with delays chosen to avoid simple ratios so their echoes do not line up into a metallic ring. Then two all-pass filters in series smear the echoes more densely in time while leaving the overall frequency balance alone:

H(z) = (−g + z−M) / (1 − g z−M)

The left and right channels use slightly different delay lengths. Those differences reach each ear separately, and that is most of what makes a mix sound wide. The reverb is 16 lines of code.

The pulse

The engine of the track is a six-note repeating pattern, given as distances in semitones from the root. It is one array:

const STEPS = [0, 12, 0, 10, 0, 7]   // root, octave, root, seventh, root, fifth

Going back to the root on every other note creates a pedal point. The first note gets the strongest accent, and the fourth gets a lesser one, which puts two gentle groups inside each bar. When the video moves to summer, a single line changes the root from D to F. The pattern stays identical, and the whole mood warms up.

The melody

Each phrase is a list of [bar, beats, MIDI note]. Here is the lament, drafted with Claude Code for this track rather than taken from any song:

lament: [[0, 3, 57], [1, 3, 58], [2, 2, 57], [2.67, 1.33, 55], [3, 3, 53], [4, 3, 52], [5, 4, 50]]

That reads A, B flat, A, G, F, E, D. It climbs one step, then walks down a whole scale to home. The B flat rubs against the A beneath it, which gives the opening its ache, and each later note steps closer to rest.

This is the one part that decides whether the track is yours. Copyright in music protects specific expression such as melodies. A mood, a metre and an instrument choice are generally free to use. So everything above can follow a theme you admire, and the notes must be your own. Asking Claude Code to reproduce a famous melody and "change it a bit" gets you an infringing track with extra steps. Write your own phrases, ideally by hand once you understand the notation above. I am not a lawyer, and automatic matching systems can still flag things, so this lowers the risk without removing it.

Following the picture

The score was written to a particular video, so it reads the edit from a file. anchors.json holds the moments that matter, in seconds:

{"now":2.02,"why":11.87,"main":17.5333,"graphics":31.413,"struck":38.037, ... "end":178.7667}

The bar length is then adjusted so that bar lines land on the title slam and on the start of the explanation, and a section() function decides how hard each bar plays:

function section(b) {
  const t = bar(b)
  if (bar(b) < MAIN - .01) return { pulse: 1, drums: "hook" }
  if (t < A.graphics) return { pulse: .45, drums: null }
  if (t < A.season) return { pulse: .65, drums: "half" }
  if (t < A.hope) return { pulse: .38, drums: null }
  return { pulse: .8, drums: "every" }
}

This is the great advantage of music as code. Change the edit and the music recomposes itself to fit. A big hit lands on the word "struck" because a line of code says drum(A.struck, .9, { decay: 1.6 }).

Part 9Finishing the audio

Three steps turn a pile of floats into a file people can play.

Normalise. The script finds the loudest sample and scales everything so that peak sits at 0.89, which is 20·log10(0.89) ≈ −1 dB below the maximum. It then rounds each value to a 16-bit integer. It skips dither, the tiny noise usually added before this rounding. That is a reasonable shortcut for a track this busy, and a good exercise to add.

Match loudness. Peak level says little about how loud something feels. A short click and a long roar can have the same peak. Loudness is measured in LUFS under the ITU-R BS.1770 standard, which applies a filter shaped like human hearing sensitivity, then averages over 400 ms blocks while ignoring silence. FFmpeg's loudnorm filter aims for a target, here −16 LUFS, while keeping peaks under −1.5 dB. In a single pass it adjusts as it goes, and on this dynamic track I measured the output at about −18.4 LUFS. A two-pass run lands closer to the target.

Compress. MP3 uses psychoacoustic masking. A loud sound hides quieter sounds close to it in frequency and time, so an encoder splits the signal into frequency bands and spends fewer bits on what you are unlikely to hear. That took the 34.9 MB WAV down to 4.4 MB. Part 1's cochlea is the reason this works so well.

Part 10Doing it with Claude Code

You could write all of this by hand. With Claude Code you describe the music and it writes the code, which means you can work at the level of composer and director, and read the code when you want to learn from it.

Know one limit before you begin. Claude cannot hear. It can check numbers: peak levels, loudness, whether a hit lands on the right sample, and it can look at a picture of the spectrum. Whether the result moves anyone is your department. So the working loop is that Claude writes and measures, you listen and describe, and it changes the code. The better your words for what you hear, the better this goes, and Parts 1 to 8 are that vocabulary.
